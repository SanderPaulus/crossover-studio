Why this exists

I am in the middle of building my next pair of speakers. The cabinet design is in its final stages — which is exactly where a build stops being woodwork and becomes a decision: go active with an FA253 or an FA251, or filter it passively.

A friend was going to help me with the passive network, but he ran out of time — and he didn't have years of experience to fall back on either. So I went looking for software, and found nothing that did what I needed.

So I asked an AI to design me a filter. Then I spent evenings rebuilding it by hand, part by part, in another program, just to find out whether it was any good.

The SPL curve I could read. The phase curve I could not — a tangle of lines that clearly meant something, and I couldn't tell what. And the software that might have helped me runs on Windows, which I don't own.

It felt like walking into a wall — and I couldn't shake the feeling that something better had to be possible. So I built it.

— Sander Somers

If you stopped in the same place

There are a lot of half-finished speakers in the world, and a lot of builds that never started at all, for one reason: the crossover.

You can cut the wood. You can choose the drivers. You can even learn to measure them. And then you reach the part where you are supposed to already know what a filter should look like — or spend an evening rebuilding someone else's suggestion in another program just to find out whether it works. That is where projects stall.

This is a bridge over that wall. It does not expect you to already know which alignment your tweeter wants or what order the filter should be. You bring the measurements and say what the design must respect; it works out where the drivers can hand over, builds and simulates every candidate, and then tells you which ones meet your requirements and why the others don't — so you can disagree with it once you know enough to.

How it goes

  1. 1

    Measure

    The hardest part is the measuring, so the app has a guide that draws your rig: how far back to stand, how high the mic sits, what a turntable sweep actually covers. The drawings and the numbers move as you change the distance, because they run on the same maths the design engine uses.

    It will tell you, for instance, that a woofer 380 mm below the microphone at half a metre is not at 0° — it is at 37°, and its "0° curve" was never on axis.

  2. 2

    Load what you measured

    A frequency response and an impedance sweep per driver, plus any angled and near-field measurements you took. One, two or three ways. No measurements yet? There is a complete real measurement set built in — the three-way this tool was developed on — so you can see the whole thing work before you own a microphone.

  3. 3

    Say what it must respect, then let it design

    You state requirements — the lowest load your amplifier may see, how hard a driver may be driven on its own resonance, how much the bass may ring — and press one button. It works out where the drivers can hand over to each other, picks the filter shape for each one, builds every candidate as a real passive network and tunes every component value against your measurements.

    What comes back is not "the best one". It is every design that meets every requirement you set, side by side — and, underneath, the ones that were refused, each with the rule that refused it. You can watch the candidates come in, and stop at any time.

  4. 4

    Go and buy the parts

    You end up with a schematic, the simulated response of the exact thing you are about to solder, and a parts list of real components — actual Jantzen and Mundorf items with their real resistance and their real price, not idealised values you then have to go and find.

    It also shows what happens to your speaker if every part is 5% off, and which parts are worth buying to tighter tolerance.

It works out what your drivers can't do

A crossover point isn't a matter of taste. It is the overlap of several hard limits, and most of them come out of your own measurements.

  • Where the woofer starts beaming

    Read from your own angled sweeps — how far the response has fallen off-axis and whether it keeps falling — instead of from the nominal size on the box.

  • Where two drivers start cancelling

    Pure geometry: drivers that sit far apart in wavelengths put a null off-axis. The app reports the spacing of each pair in wavelengths, but it only judges the thing you would hear — the summed response over a vertical window of angles, built from the acoustic centres you entered.

  • Where a cone resonance gets excited

    A breakup at 5 kHz is fed by everything you send it at 1.7 kHz. The price lands well over an octave below the peak, where a notch can't reach it.

  • How hard a driver may be driven on its resonance

    From your amplifier's peak power, the driver's excursion limit, its motor strength and moving mass, and the resonance read from your impedance sweep — not from a listening level you have to guess. Excursion is one failure mode; for a dome the binding limit is usually a stated convention, and the app shows both side by side and applies the stricter one.

The usable window is where all of them overlap — and the app names which limit is binding, so you know what to change. If there is no window at all, it says that too, instead of quietly designing something impossible.

It tells you what it will not judge

Every requirement is yours to state: the lowest impedance your amplifier may see, the dissipation you accept in the filter, how hard a driver may be driven on its resonance, how much the bass may ring around the port resonance, how far the filter may raise the woofer's Q, how deep the bass plateau sits below the rest.

What you do not state is not judged — and the app says so, in the report and next to every gate: off when no limit is set, not judged when it could not look, inside or exceeded when it did. There are no hidden defaults that quietly take part. The suggestions in the empty fields are ghost text; they never reach the engine.

That also means a run with nothing stated is a run with nothing enforced. The report still shows every number, because a value you cannot see is a value you cannot judge.

The shortlist

A run ends in a list, not a winner. Every row is a complete design that meets every requirement and every gate you set — a different topology, tuned, priced. The list is ordered by how flat the result is against your target curve, and you can sort it on any column; sorting changes nothing about which designs are on it. A view, not a verdict. The choice is yours.

Underneath are the candidates that delivered nothing: their tune was refused as a whole by a protection rule, and the row says which one. They are not near-misses and they cannot be loaded — nobody looked at a design there, and the app will not pretend otherwise.

Click a row and that design lands in the working tab and in every chart. Click another and compare.

It knows where the measurement stops

A gated measurement is only valid above the frequency its own window allows, and that number is in the file's header. The app reads it and derives the crossover window from it: the floor is the higher of where both measurements can still be believed and where the upper driver's resonance sits, the ceiling is where the lower driver breaks up or starts beaming — and it names which one binds. Nothing is proposed below the floor, ever.

The older physics floor — resonance, the near-field splice — is shown next to it as a second opinion, with its source. The two answer different questions, and the app does not silently pick one because it came first in the pipeline. Before a run it counts how many candidates would fall outside the window and says so; it never clamps your range.

And if a measurement file has lost its gate header, the app will not trust its own level anchor: it says which input is missing and waits for you to supply the window time, rather than computing a confident number on a band it cannot vouch for.

The idea underneath all of it

Two drivers only sum properly if their phase lines up where they overlap — and part of that phase is simply the fact that one driver's acoustic centre sits a couple of centimetres deeper in the cabinet than the other's.

This tool designs on the phase you actually measured, that real time difference included. It checks first whether your sweeps even share a clock, and says so plainly if they don't, because that single mistake silently ruins every phase number downstream.

A phase plot is also the one graph most people cannot read, so the app does not just draw it. It scores it, and it colours the summed response by it: green where the two drivers are within 15° of each other, red where they are fighting. You can see whether the crossover works before you understand why.

It was tested against a real speaker

This was built on one real three-way project with real measurements: two woofers, a mid, a tweeter, measured on the finished cabinet. Every claim the engine makes about those measurements — every derived resonance, every window, every gate value — is pinned to a reference and checked by a test suite of over 1,500 tests, part of which runs on a different machine and operating system with every deploy. When a number moves, the suite says so.

On that speaker, given the same topology as the filter I designed by hand, the optimizer reaches the flatness of that hand-made filter. And the hand-made filter is held to the same requirements as everything the engine proposes; it passes them, which is how I know the requirements are achievable rather than decorative.

It is one speaker. Whether that design gets built passively or as a hybrid with active bass is not decided yet — the app exists to make that decision with numbers instead of hope, and that is how I use it.

Before you get your hopes up

  • You do need measurements. This turns measurements into a crossover; it can't conjure them. A measurement microphone and REW or ARTA will get you there, and the guide in the app walks through the rig.
  • Your measurement files need their gate header. The window the measurement was taken with is what tells the app where the data stops being valid. Export with the header intact, or type the window times in by hand; without either, the app will refuse to anchor levels rather than guess.
  • A full run takes a while. One candidate is minutes of tuning; a whole field can take up to an hour in the browser. You can watch it, and you can stop it.
  • The excursion limit needs your datasheet. Deriving how hard a driver may be driven takes its excursion limit, motor strength and moving mass, plus your amplifier's peak power. Leave them out and that derivation is simply off — the report says so — and the stated limit alone judges. A second, acoustic check of the same number needs the voltage your response was measured at; if that was never written down, that route stays off, with the reason shown.
  • It is a test version, and it is being worked on. Things move. If something looks wrong, it probably is, and I'd like to hear about it.
  • The manual is in English and Dutch, and follows the language you pick in the app.
  • Nothing leaves your browser. No account, no upload, no server. Your work is saved locally; save a project file to keep or share it.
  • Designs export to VituixCAD, so nothing you do in here is locked in.
  • There is nothing to install, and no Windows required. It is a web page: it runs on whatever you already have. Chrome or Edge if you want the folder export; everything else works in any modern browser.
