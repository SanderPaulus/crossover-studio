Why this exists

I am in the middle of building my next pair of speakers. The cabinet design is in its final stages — which is exactly where a build stops being woodwork and becomes a decision: go active with an FA253, or filter it passively.

A friend was going to help me with the passive network, but he ran out of time — and he didn't have years of experience to fall back on either. So I went looking for software, and found nothing that did what I needed.

So I asked an AI to design me a filter. Then I spent evenings rebuilding it by hand, part by part, in another program, just to find out whether it was any good.

The SPL curve I could read. The phase curve I could not — a tangle of lines that clearly meant something, and I couldn't tell what. And the software that might have helped me runs on Windows, which I don't own.

It felt like walking into a wall — and I couldn't shake the feeling that something better had to be possible. So I built it.

— Sander Somers

If you stopped in the same place

There are a lot of half-finished speakers in the world, and a lot of builds that never started at all, for one reason: the crossover.

You can cut the wood. You can choose the drivers. You can even learn to measure them. And then you reach the part where you are supposed to already know what a filter should look like — or spend an evening rebuilding someone else's suggestion in another program just to find out whether it works. That is where projects stall.

This is a bridge over that wall. It does not expect you to already know which alignment your tweeter wants or what order the filter should be. You bring the measurements; it decides, builds, simulates — and then tells you what it decided and why, so you can disagree with it once you know enough to.

How it goes

  1. 1

    Measure

    The hardest part is the measuring, so the app has a guide that draws your rig: how far back to stand, how high the mic sits, what a turntable sweep actually covers. The drawings and the numbers move as you change the distance, because they run on the same maths the design engine uses.

    It will tell you, for instance, that a woofer 380 mm below the microphone at half a metre is not at 0° — it is at 37°, and its "0° curve" was never on axis.

  2. 2

    Load what you measured

    A frequency response and an impedance sweep per driver, plus any angled measurements you took. One, two or three ways. No measurements yet? There is a complete real measurement set built in, so you can see the whole thing work before you own a microphone.

  3. 3

    Let it design

    One button. It works out where the drivers should hand over to each other, picks the filter shape for each one, builds the result as a real passive network, tunes every component value against your measurements and keeps the best of nine complete designs.

    You can watch each candidate come in, and stop it at any time. Nothing is hidden behind the button: every choice it made is written out afterwards.

  4. 4

    Go and buy the parts

    You end up with a schematic, the simulated response of the exact thing you are about to solder, and a parts list of real components — actual Jantzen and Mundorf items with their real resistance and their real price, not idealised values you then have to go and find.

    It also shows what happens to your speaker if every part is 5% off, and which parts are worth buying to tighter tolerance.

It works out what your drivers can't do

A crossover point isn't a matter of taste. It is the overlap of several hard limits, and most of them come out of your own measurements.

  • Where the woofer starts beaming

    Read from your own angled sweeps — how far the response has fallen off-axis and whether it keeps falling — instead of from the nominal size on the box.

  • Where two drivers start cancelling

    Pure geometry: once they sit further apart than half a wavelength, a null exists off-axis. This is the real reason three-ways cross low.

  • Where a cone resonance gets excited

    A breakup at 5 kHz is fed by everything you send it at 1.7 kHz. The price lands well over an octave below the peak, where a notch can't reach it.

  • How low it can play at your level

    From cone area and excursion: the same one-inch dome that survives 587 Hz at 90 dB needs 829 Hz at 96 dB.

The usable window is where all of them overlap — and the app names which limit is binding, so you know what to change. If there is no window at all, it says that too, instead of quietly designing something impossible.

The idea underneath all of it

Two drivers only sum properly if their phase lines up where they overlap — and part of that phase is simply the fact that one driver's acoustic centre sits a couple of centimetres deeper in the cabinet than the other's.

This tool designs on the phase you actually measured, that real time difference included. It checks first whether your two sweeps even share a clock, and says so plainly if they don't, because that single mistake silently ruins every phase number downstream.

A phase plot is also the one graph most people cannot read, so the app does not just draw it. It scores it, and it colours the summed response by it: green where the two drivers are within 15° of each other, red where they are fighting. You can see whether the crossover works before you understand why.

Before you get your hopes up

  • You do need measurements. This turns measurements into a crossover; it can't conjure them. A measurement microphone and REW will get you there, and the guide in the app walks through the rig.
  • It is a test version, and it is being worked on. Things move. If something looks wrong, it probably is, and I'd like to hear about it.
  • The written manual is currently in Dutch, even though the app itself is in English. Translation is on the list.
  • Nothing leaves your browser. No account, no upload, no server. Your work is saved locally; save a project file to keep or share it.
  • Designs export to VituixCAD, so nothing you do in here is locked in.
  • There is nothing to install, and no Windows required. It is a web page: it runs on whatever you already have. Chrome or Edge if you want the folder export; everything else works in any modern browser.
